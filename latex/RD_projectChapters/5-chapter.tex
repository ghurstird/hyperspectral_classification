\subsection{Performance Change Analysis}
Accuracy change relative to the supervised baseline is represented by the following:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Change\%} = \frac{\text{OA}_{\text{alt}} - \text{OA}_{\text{Supervised}}}{\text{OA}_{\text{Supervised}}} \times 100
    \label{eq:improvement}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Semi-Supervised vs. Supervised}
Given the scale of the datasets, semi-supervised learning degrades significantly in comparison to supervised learning.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Indian Pines:} Average drop of $-34.96$ percentages across architectures (from 99.81\% to 64.85\%). This drop shows that self training is considerably limited by dataset size and class balance.
    \item \textbf{Pavia University:} Average drop of only $-4.63$ percentages (from 99.91\% to 95.28\%). This drop shows that in the presence of large uncontrolled dataset curriculum learning helps to limit the degradation.
    \item \textbf{Salinas:} Average drop of $-6.46$ percentages (from 99.88\% to 93.42\%). From this drop, we see that the largest dataset in the study is still insufficient to reach fully-accurate, supervised-level in the presence of pseudo-labels.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Federated vs. Supervised}
When compared to the other approaches, supervised learning aggregates at the ceiling level more than the others, especially in the large datasets:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Indian Pines:} Average difference of $-8.26$ percentages (from 99.81\% to 91.55\%), showing the difficulty federated aggregation has on small datasets with limited local samples for each client.
    \item \textbf{Pavia University:} Average difference of just $-0.40$ percentages (from 99.91\% to 99.51\%), which shows strong robustness.
    \item \textbf{Salinas:} Average difference of just $-0.75$ percentage points (from 99.88\% to 99.13\%), showing near-parity at large scale.
\end{itemize}

Three trends emerge based on averages across different architectures.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{2D-CNN shows dominance across the board:}  As the most basic architecture, 2D-CNN achieves the highest score in all paradigms—supervised (99.96\%), semi-supervised (89.22\%), and federated (98.72\%)—where PCA reduces the spectral dimension to 30 components. Class separation seems to require only 2D spatial operations. 3D convolutions add complexity to the architecture without providing a significant increase in accuracy.

    \item \textbf{3D-CNN shows reliability:} The 3D-CNN is in second place across the three paradigms (supervised: 99.91\%, semi-supervised: 86.92\%, federated: 97.36\%). The stable performance is attributed to the 3D-CNN’s design of volumetric kernels, which model inter-band relations.

    \item \textbf{Distribution shift most affects ViT:} The Vision Transformer shows strong performance in the supervised paradigm (99.72\%) but experiences the largest drop in semi-supervised (77.41\%) and federated (94.11\%) paradigms. Of the architectures, ViTs are the most affected by inadequate quality of pseudo-labels and the averaging of model weights across heterogeneous client data.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dataset-Specific Analysis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Indian Pines:} The class distribution (16 classes) and sample size (10,249 samples) make this the most difficult case for semi-supervised and federated learning. Semi-supervised accuracy dropped on average 34.96 (99.81\% $\to$ 64.85\%) due to the propagation of labeling errors on the pseudo-labels of severely underrepresented classes.

    \item \textbf{Pavia University:} With 42,776 samples covering 9 classes, this is a dataset that shows stability against paradigm changes. Federated learning accuracy decreased by only 0.40 percentage points, indicating that this architecture is applicable to scenarios where urban remote sensing data is distributed across organizations.

    \item \textbf{Salinas:} The largest dataset (54,129 samples) achieves the highest semi-supervised accuracy of any configuration (3D-CNN + SSL: 97.30\%) and the smallest federated-supervised gap ($-0.75$ percentage points). It shows that both different frameworks work well when the labeled and/or the unlabeled sample sizes increase.
\end{itemize}
